I have read the paper “A space of inference spaces in the space sciences”. Overall,
I found it a useful and valuable addition to the literature that shows the diversity
of targets encountered in statistical inference in physical sciences; classifies them
by pathologies; and shows the performance of various computer packages on
them. I think it could be improved by more careful definitions in some places,
and more connections to existing literature. I liked the speculative remarks
about the future: that providing these databases of problems could spur future
developments.

For existing literature,
• posteriordb: see https://github.com/stan-dev/posteriordb and https:
//proceedings.mlr.press/v258/magnusson25a.html
• https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30412
• https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18464
• Referring explicitly to Simulation Based Calibration when you cite the Gelman et al paper
• Refer to any standard measures of measures of modality, non-Gaussianity etc if they exist in the literature

For the definitions of the pathologies,
• Modes was based on density and thus isn’t invariant under reparameterization; what parameterization does this definition use? Is there a canonical way to define and count modes in high-dimensions sample-based representations of a density?
• For non-Gaussianity, the statement of BvM theorem could be refined. While the measure of non-Gaussianity itself, the KL divergence, is independent of choice of prameterization, one must choose a parmaeterisation under which the reference measure is a Gaussian and under which one
estimates the µ and Σ.
• I had trouble with tail weight or width. I would have anticipated
(V_{95}/V_5)^{1/d} for a characteristic measure of width; in log-space
TW = (1/d) * log(V_{95}/V_5).
This differs from the TW definition used in the paper. I’m confused about how this is supposed to interplay with the curse of dimensionality — in highdimensions, more and more probability mass lies away from the mode. The tail weight increases with dimensionality; the − log d term seems to try to prevent this. Should it? I think further discussion and justification of this as a measure of TW pathology would be helpful. 
In any case, these mean be high-probability mass tails, but they aren’t necessarily heavy tails. As the author points out, heavy tails are characterizing by the rate of decay of the probability density.

• For the phase transitions, a measure of the strength of the phase transition
is introduced. I wondered if Fig. 3 was supposed to be log-likelihood
and log-volume. In simulated annealing approaches, we expect posterior
samples to be concentrated at
d log L(X) / d logX = -1/β
which for β = 1 gives the -1 gradient mentioning in the text. I could understand something like the difference between the area under the convex hull of the log L versus log X, and the area under of log L versus log X curve, as a measure of nonconvexity:
\int log L(X) - (log L(X))^{hull} d log X
There is even an analogy to thermodynamics here. We could measure the
strength of a first-order phase transition for potential V by
\int V (ϕ) - V^{hull}(ϕ) dϕ
where hull indicates convex hull. In a first order phase transition, there is a
barrier such that the potential is non-convex. And this is a measure of the size of area of that barrier, something like the height time width of the barrier.

However, in the text we have (changing notation to avoid a clash):
\frac{\int L(X)^{hull} dX}{\int L(X) dX}
I find this one hard to understand or justify as a measure of the strength of a
phase transition. Some more discussion here would be helpful.

Similarly, inspired by thermodynamics, one could take ∆ log X — the distance
between the two modes in a plot of the posterior P (log X).
